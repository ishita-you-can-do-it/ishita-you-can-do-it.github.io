## Question 1

### 1. Question

Computer assisted testing is principally based on _________.

### 2. Options

(A) Item response theory
(B) Efficiency of modern computers
(C) Emphasis on internal consistency
(D) Compatibility between manual and computerised scoring

### 3. Answer

**A** — as listed in the supplied answer key.

### 4. Explanation

Computer-assisted testing (CAT) is an assessment delivered and scored through a computer. The full conceptual explanation and connected learning points follow below.

### 5. Relevant concepts and exam connections

Computer-assisted testing (CAT) is an assessment delivered and scored through a computer. In adaptive CAT, item response theory (IRT) estimates a candidate's ability after each response and selects the next item to provide maximum information near that ability level. It is not simply digitised paper testing. IRT models item characteristics such as difficulty, discrimination and guessing; the one-, two- and three-parameter logistic models progressively include these features. Advantages include tailored difficulty, efficient testing and immediate scoring. Distinguish computer-based testing (fixed item set) from computer-adaptive testing (item selection changes according to responses).

## Question 2

### 1. Question

Identify the plausible logic for the introduction of deviation IQ.

### 2. Options

(A) Level of ratio IQ varies with age
(B) Ratio IQ is not measured on interval scale
(C) SD of ratio IQ varies scale - to - scale
(D) Comparison of ratio IQ at different age levels is not possible as SD of IQ is not constant

### 3. Answer

**D** — as listed in the supplied answer key.

### 4. Explanation

Ratio IQ = (mental age / chronological age) × 100. The full conceptual explanation and connected learning points follow below.

### 5. Relevant concepts and exam connections

Ratio IQ = (mental age / chronological age) × 100. It becomes problematic across age groups because mental-age differences do not have the same meaning at every age and its distribution/variability is not suitably constant. Deviation IQ locates a person's performance relative to same-age peers on a standard scale, commonly mean 100 and SD 15. A z score is (X−M)/SD; IQ = 100 + 15z in the common Wechsler metric. Thus a deviation IQ is norm-referenced and supports comparison across age groups. Remember: ratio IQ is a ratio-age concept; deviation IQ is a standardized, age-normed score.

## Question 3

### 1. Question

At which difficulty level (pass proportion) the item can make highest differentiation?

### 2. Options

(A) 0.35
(B) 0.50
(C) 0.75
(D) 0.25

### 3. Answer

**B** — as listed in the supplied answer key.

### 4. Explanation

For a dichotomously scored item, difficulty (p) is the proportion answering correctly. The full conceptual explanation and connected learning points follow below.

### 5. Relevant concepts and exam connections

For a dichotomously scored item, difficulty (p) is the proportion answering correctly. An item with p near .50 generally provides the greatest variance and, under common assumptions, the strongest discrimination between higher- and lower-ability examinees. p near 1.00 is very easy; p near 0 is very difficult. The complementary q = 1−p, and item variance is pq, maximised at p=.50. Difficulty is not the same as discrimination: discrimination concerns how well an item separates high- and low-performing candidates (e.g., point-biserial correlation or upper–lower group index).

## Question 5

### 1. Question

When an experimenter wishes to ensure equal representation of a highly correlated
variable with dependent variable at all levels of independent variable, he/she should use
_______design.

### 2. Options

(A) Repeated measures
(B) Randomized groups
(C) Latin Square
(D) Randomized block

### 3. Answer

**D** — as listed in the supplied answer key.

### 4. Explanation

A randomized-block design controls a nuisance variable that is strongly related to the dependent variable. The full conceptual explanation and connected learning points follow below.

### 5. Relevant concepts and exam connections

A randomized-block design controls a nuisance variable that is strongly related to the dependent variable. Participants are grouped into relatively homogeneous blocks on that variable, then randomly assigned to treatment conditions within each block. This reduces error variance and improves precision. Example: block students by baseline ability, then allocate within each ability block to teaching methods. Repeated-measures design exposes the same participants to all conditions; randomized-groups design assigns different participants to conditions without blocking. Latin square controls two nuisance factors through balanced orderings, often for order/carryover effects.

The human retina contains approximately 120 million rods and 6 million cones. ## Question 23

### 1. Question

A researcher using a group of ten subjects under four conditions repeatedly, what will be
degree of freedom for the error terms?

### 2. Options

(A) 9
(B) 27
(C) 36
(D) 39

### 3. Answer

**B** — as listed in the supplied answer key.

### 4. Explanation

For a one-factor repeated-measures design with n=10 participants across k=4 conditions, the error degrees of freedom are (n−1)(k−1)=9×3=27. The full conceptual explanation and connected learning points follow below.

### 5. Relevant concepts and exam connections

For a one-factor repeated-measures design with n=10 participants across k=4 conditions, the error degrees of freedom are (n−1)(k−1)=9×3=27. The condition df is k−1=3 and participant df is n−1=9. Repeated-measures designs use the same people in every condition, controlling stable individual differences, but require attention to order effects and the sphericity assumption. If sphericity is violated, Greenhouse–Geisser or Huynh–Feldt corrections adjust degrees of freedom.
