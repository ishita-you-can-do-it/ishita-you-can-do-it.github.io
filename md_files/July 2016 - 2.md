## Questions and explanations

### Question 1

**Question as printed:**

```text
1. A procedure that establishes a sequence of responses which lead to a reward following
   the final response and then working backwards is called

         (1)    Shaping                                    (2)    Stimulus control
         (3)    Chaining                                   (4)    Stimulus
                                                                  generalization
```

**Printed answer key:** **3**.

#### Explanation and connected concepts

**Correct answer: (3) Chaining.** Chaining teaches a sequence made of several linked responses. In backward chaining, the trainer first teaches the final response in the sequence, then adds the preceding response, progressively working backwards until the learner performs the whole chain. The final response contacts the reward early in training.

**Distinguish the terms:** *Shaping* reinforces successive approximations of one target response; *chaining* links separately identifiable responses into a sequence; *stimulus control* means a response occurs reliably in the presence of a discriminative stimulus; *stimulus generalisation* is responding similarly to stimuli resembling the trained stimulus.

```text
2. In a study on scaling of attitude items, eleven statements were included. What would be
   the number of pairs of attitude statements ?

         (1)    21                                         (2)    55
         (3) 110                                           (4) 12
```

**Printed answer key:** **2**.

#### Explanation and connected concepts

**Correct answer: (2) 55.** Paired comparison presents every possible pair of statements to judges. The number of unique pairs among \(n\) statements is \(n(n-1)/2\). For 11 statements: \(11×10÷2=55\).

**Exam connection:** For 5 statements there are 10 pairs; for 10 statements, 45; for 20 statements, 190. The number grows quadratically, which makes paired comparison burdensome for large item sets.

### Question 4

**Question as printed:**

```text
4. Read each of the following two statements – Assertion (A) and Reason (R) and indicate
   your answer using codes given below :
  Assertion (A) :         Classical conditioning is quick when UCS is presented immediately
   after CS rather than before.
  Reason (R)          : According to cognitive perspective, classical conditioning occurs
   because CS provides expectancy about the coming of UCS.

  Codes :
  (1) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  (2) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  (3) (A) is true, but (R) is false.

     (4) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
```

**Printed answer key:** **1**.

#### Explanation and connected concepts

**Correct answer: (1).** Both statements are true, and the reason gives a cognitive account of why the temporal relation matters. In forward-delay conditioning, the conditioned stimulus (CS) begins before the unconditioned stimulus (US/UCS) and predicts its arrival. A reliable predictive relation supports learning; backward conditioning, in which the US comes first, is usually less effective.

**Conditioning arrangements:** delayed (CS overlaps and continues into US), trace (CS ends before US, leaving a gap), simultaneous (CS and US begin together), and backward (US precedes CS). The cognitive expectancy account emphasises information/prediction, not mere temporal contiguity.

**Correct answer: (2) a, c and d only.** Freud's motivational account includes drives/instincts and the pleasure principle (often described as hedonistic). ### Question 7

**Question as printed:**

```text
7. Which of the following is correctly arranged ?

(1) Encoding – retrieval  – storage

(2) Encoding – storage – retrieval
(3) Storage – encoding – retrieval
(4) Retrieval – encoding – storage
```

**Printed answer key:** **2**.

#### Explanation and connected concepts

**Correct answer: (2) Encoding → storage → retrieval.** Memory is commonly described in three broad processes. Encoding transforms incoming information into a form that can be registered; storage maintains it over time; retrieval accesses stored information when needed.

**Examples:** encoding a phone number by repeating or grouping it; storage retaining it; retrieval recalling it later. Failure can occur at any stage: weak attention/encoding, loss or interference during retention, or retrieval failure despite stored information being available.

**Correct answer: (3) Gender** according to the printed key. ### Question 10

**Question as printed:**

```text
10. In retrograde amnesia, which of the following kind of problem is seen :

(1) Problems with recalling previously learned material
(2) Problems with face recognition.
(3) Difficulty finding routes.
(4) Poor encoding of verbal material.
```

**Printed answer key:** **1**.

#### Explanation and connected concepts

**Correct answer: (1) Problems with recalling previously learned material.** Retrograde amnesia is loss or impairment of memories formed before the event or injury that caused amnesia. It differs from anterograde amnesia, which is difficulty forming new long-term memories after the onset.

**Memory distinctions:** episodic memory concerns personally experienced events; semantic memory concerns facts and general knowledge; procedural memory concerns skills. Amnesia can affect these systems unevenly. Retrograde loss may show a temporal gradient, with more recent memories often more vulnerable.

```text
16. Lesions of the ventromedial nuclei in the hypothalamus produce

(1) Anorexia                                                   (2) Obesity
(3) Low level of Leptin                                         (4) Hyperinsulinemia
```

**Printed answer key:** **2**.

**Correct answer: (2) Obesity.** The ventromedial hypothalamic (VMH) region has classically been associated with satiety. ### Question 18

**Question as printed:**

```text
18. When the action potential reaches the end of the axon terminals, it causes the release
 of

(1) An electric spark that sets off the next neuron
(2) Positively charged ions that excite the next cell
(3) Negatively charged ions that inhibit the next cell.
(4) Neurotransmitters that excite or inhibit the next cell.
```

**Printed answer key:** **4**.

#### Explanation and connected concepts

**Correct answer: (4) Neurotransmitters that excite or inhibit the next cell.** When an action potential reaches the presynaptic terminal, voltage-gated calcium channels open. ### Question 25

**Question as printed:**

```text
25. Read each of the following two statements – Assertion (A) and Reason (R) and indicate

your answer using codes given below :
Assertion (A) : According to Classical Test Theory (CTT), reliability can be conceptualized as
 the ratio of true score variance to the observed score variance.
Reason (R) : According to the CTT, the random error of measurement is assumed to be
 uncorrelated with the true score.
Codes :
 (1) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
(2) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
(3) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
(4) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
```

**Printed answer key:** **1**.

#### Explanation and connected concepts

**Correct answer: (1).** Both statements are true, and the reason supports the assertion within Classical Test Theory (CTT). CTT represents an observed score as true score plus measurement error: \(X=T+E\). If random error is uncorrelated with true score, observed-score variance can be partitioned into true-score and error variance, and reliability is \(Var(T)/Var(X)\).

**Key formulas:** \(X=T+E\); reliability \(r_{xx}=\sigma_T^2/\sigma_X^2\); and, under the classical assumptions, \(\sigma_X^2=\sigma_T^2+\sigma_E^2\). Reliability concerns score consistency/precision, not validity.

## High-yield topic-wise revision guide

### Learning, conditioning and memory
- **Chaining** links a sequence of responses; **shaping** reinforces successive approximations; **stimulus control** is responding in the presence of a discriminative stimulus; **generalisation** extends responding to similar stimuli.
- Classical conditioning arrangements: delayed (CS precedes and overlaps US), trace (CS ends before US), simultaneous (CS and US begin together), backward (US precedes CS). Forward-delay arrangements are generally most effective.
- Operant consequences: positive reinforcement adds a stimulus and increases behaviour; negative reinforcement removes/prevents an aversive stimulus and increases behaviour; punishment decreases behaviour; extinction withholds reinforcement.
- Memory processes: encoding → storage → retrieval. Retrograde amnesia affects memories from before onset; anterograde amnesia impairs formation of new memories.
- Recall produces information, recognition identifies previously encountered information, relearning shows savings, and reintegrative memory uses cues to reconstruct personal events.
- Elaborative rehearsal and meaningful encoding support long-term learning; maintenance rehearsal can keep information active in short-term memory but does not guarantee durable storage.

### Personality, motivation and intelligence
- Lexical trait research: Allport and Odbert identified personality descriptors; Cattell used factor analysis to organise traits; Costa and McCrae developed the NEO/Five-Factor inventories.
- Freud's instinct/drive components: **source, aim, impetus and object**. Eysenck's PEN dimensions are Psychoticism, Extraversion and Neuroticism.
- Guilford's SOI model: **operations × contents × products**. Products: units, classes, relations, systems, transformations and implications.
- Fluid intelligence (gf) supports novel reasoning and tends to decline with age; crystallized intelligence (gc) reflects acquired knowledge and often remains stable or increases into adulthood.
- The historical ratio IQ formula is \(IQ=(MA/CA)×100\). Modern adult tests generally use deviation IQ scores, commonly normed around mean 100 and SD 15.
- Masters and Johnson's sexual-response phases: excitement → plateau → orgasm → resolution. Yerkes–Dodson describes an often inverted-U relation between arousal and performance.

### Biological, sensory and physiological psychology
- Synaptic transmission: action potential reaches terminal → calcium enters → vesicles release neurotransmitter → receptor activation produces excitatory or inhibitory effects.
- Middle-ear ossicles transmit vibrations to the oval window; deterioration can cause conductive hearing impairment.
- Light pathway through the eye: cornea → pupil → lens → retina. Rods support dim-light vision; cones support colour and fine detail.
- Trichromatic theory: S-, M- and L-cones (often simplified as blue, green and red). Opponent-process theory explains opponent colour channels.
- Binocular disparity compares the two retinal images; convergence uses inward eye movement; accommodation changes lens shape. These are distinct depth cues.
- Sympathetic activation supports fight-or-flight (increased pulse, perspiration and pupil dilation; reduced digestion); parasympathetic activity supports rest-and-digest functions.
- Classical hypothalamic account: lateral hypothalamus is associated with feeding; ventromedial region with satiety. Modern feeding regulation is distributed and hormonally modulated.

### Social, developmental and cognitive psychology
- Inductive reasoning generalises from observations; deductive reasoning applies general premises to specific cases; analogical reasoning transfers relations across situations.
- Artificial concepts are rule-defined; natural concepts are organised around family resemblance/prototypes; prototypes are typical category members.
- The Müller–Lyer illusion is linked in the carpentered-world hypothesis to experience with rectangular corners and linear perspective cues.
- Mental chemistry is associated with John Stuart Mill: simple ideas may combine into complex ideas in which the original elements lose separate identity.
- Stem cells self-renew and differentiate; embryonic stem cells are pluripotent, while many adult stem cells are multipotent.

### Research methods, measurement and psychometrics
- Number of paired comparisons: \(n(n-1)/2\). For 11 statements, there are 55 unique pairs.
- Classical Test Theory: \(X=T+E\); reliability \(r_{xx}=Var(T)/Var(X)\), assuming error is uncorrelated with true score.
- Item-total correlation includes the item in the total; corrected item–remainder correlation excludes it and reduces part–whole inflation.
- Attitude scaling: paired comparison (Thurstone); equal-appearing intervals (judge-placed categories/scale values); scalogram (Guttman, coefficient of reproducibility); summated ratings (Likert, item analysis).
- Experimental variables: independent variable is manipulated; dependent variable is measured; controlled variables are held constant; organismic variables are participant characteristics.
- Random assignment allocates participants to conditions and helps balance extraneous differences; it is distinct from random sampling and from manipulation of the independent variable.
- A directional hypothesis specifies the expected direction; a causal hypothesis predicts an effect of a manipulated independent variable on an outcome.
