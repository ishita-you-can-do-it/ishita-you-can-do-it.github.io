## Question 1

### 1. Question and options

```text
1. The term 'integral yoga' is popularized by:
   (A) Panini
   (B) Patanjali
   (C) Sri Aurobindo
   (D) Ramakrishna Paramahansa
```

### 2. Answer

**C**

### 3. Explanation

Integral Yoga was popularised by Sri Aurobindo. It aims at an integral transformation of human nature, bringing physical, vital, mental and spiritual dimensions into a unified yoga. Contrast this with Patanjali's eight-limbed Ashtanga Yoga and Vivekananda's modern exposition of Raja Yoga.

### 4. Relevant concepts and exam connections

**Integral Yoga — meaning and framework**
- **Sri Aurobindo (1872–1950)** developed Integral Yoga, also called Purna Yoga. Its aim is not merely liberation from ordinary life but transformation of the whole human being and life through spiritual realisation.
- Aurobindo's human nature framework commonly distinguishes the **physical** (body), **vital** (life-force, desires, emotions), **mental** (thought and reasoning) and **spiritual** dimensions. The aspiration is an integral transformation rather than development of only one faculty.
- **The Mother (Mirra Alfassa)** collaborated in the development of the spiritual community at Auroville and the Sri Aurobindo Ashram.
- **Do not confuse:** Patanjali is associated with the eight limbs of Yoga (Ashtanga Yoga); Swami Vivekananda popularised Raja Yoga in the modern period; Ramakrishna is associated with spiritual universalism; Panini with Sanskrit grammar.
- **Exam link:** Match Indian thinkers with their contributions: Aurobindo—Integral Yoga; Patanjali—Yoga Sutras/eight-limbed path; Panini—Ashtadhyayi; Vivekananda—Raja Yoga and Vedanta.

## Question 2

### 1. Question and options

```text
2. Which of the following correlation coefficient represents the variables with the weakest degree of
   relationship?
   (A) - . 84
   (B) - . 56
   (C) + . 75
   (D) + . 08
```

### 2. Answer

**D**

### 3. Explanation

Correlation strength is determined by the absolute value of r. +0.08 is closest to zero and therefore weakest; the sign only indicates direction. Pearson's r ranges from -1 to +1. Correlation does not establish causation.

### 4. Relevant concepts and exam connections

**Correlation coefficient (r)**
- Correlation measures the **direction and strength of association** between two variables. Pearson's product-moment coefficient ranges from **−1 to +1**.
- The **sign** gives direction: positive means variables tend to increase together; negative means one tends to decrease as the other increases. The **absolute magnitude** gives strength. Values close to zero indicate weak *linear* association; ±1 indicates a perfect linear relationship.
- Illustrations: r = −.84 is strong negative; r = +.75 is strong positive; r = +.08 is very weak positive. Strength conventions (small/moderate/large) are contextual, not universal cut-offs.
- **Related terms:** covariance, scatterplot, coefficient of determination (r²), restricted range, outliers, nonlinear association, spurious correlation. r² is the proportion of variance shared/explained in a simple linear model.
- **Exam trap:** r = −.90 is stronger than r = +.40, despite being negative. Correlation does not establish causation.

## Question 3

### 1. Question and options

```text
3. If observers disagree 15 times out of 75, then the inter-rater reliability is:
   (A) 50%
   (B) 80%
   (C) 20%
   (D) 60%
```

### 2. Answer

**B**

### 3. Explanation

Agreement = 75 - 15 = 60. Inter-rater percentage agreement = 60/75 × 100 = 80%. This simple index does not correct for chance agreement; Cohen's kappa does.

### 4. Relevant concepts and exam connections

**Inter-rater reliability**
- Reliability is the consistency of measurement. Inter-rater (inter-observer) reliability is the degree to which two or more independent observers assign the same ratings/codes to the same behaviour or material.
- **Percentage agreement** = (number of agreements ÷ total ratings) × 100. Here: (75 − 15)/75 × 100 = 80%.
- Percentage agreement is easy to calculate but can overestimate reliability because some agreement occurs by chance. **Cohen's kappa (κ)** adjusts for chance agreement: κ = (observed agreement − chance agreement)/(1 − chance agreement). Weighted kappa is used for ordered categories.
- **Intraclass correlation (ICC)** is often used for continuous ratings; Krippendorff's alpha can handle multiple raters and different measurement levels.
- **Improve agreement:** operationally define categories, train raters, use a coding manual, conduct pilot coding, and resolve ambiguous rules before formal data collection.

## Question 4

### 1. Question and options

```text
4. If a student scored 12 on a test, of which the mean is 16 and the standard deviation is 4, what is his
   Z-score?
   (A) - 1.0
   (B) + 1.0
   (C) + 3.0
   (D) 0.0
```

### 2. Answer

**A**

### 3. Explanation

z = (X - μ)/σ = (12 - 16)/4 = -1. A negative z-score means the score is below the mean. A z-score expresses distance from the mean in standard-deviation units.

### 4. Relevant concepts and exam connections

**Z-scores and standard scores**
- A z-score tells how many standard deviations a score lies above or below the mean: **z = (X − μ)/σ** (or (X − M)/SD for sample notation).
- z = 0 is the mean; positive z is above the mean; negative z is below. In this item, (12−16)/4 = −1, one SD below the mean.
- For a normal distribution: about 68% of scores lie within ±1 SD, 95% within ±1.96 SD, and 99.7% within ±3 SD (68–95–99.7 rule). These are approximations.
- **T-score:** T = 50 + 10z (mean 50, SD 10). **IQ deviation scores** commonly have mean 100 and SD 15, though tests differ. Percentile rank indicates the percentage at or below a score; it is not the same as percentage correct.
- **Exam trap:** Standardisation makes scores comparable across scales; it does not make the underlying tests identical.

```text
5. Which one is the correct formula for variance?

                         𝑁𝑁

   (D) Variance =
                          𝑁𝑁
```

### 2. Answer

**B**

### 3. Explanation

Population variance is σ² = Σ(X - μ)²/N. Sample variance usually divides by n - 1. Standard deviation is the square root of variance. Variance is expressed in squared units.

### 4. Relevant concepts and exam connections

**Variance and standard deviation**
- Variance describes dispersion: the average squared distance of observations from their mean. Population variance: **σ² = Σ(X−μ)²/N**. Sample variance: **s² = Σ(X−M)²/(n−1)**, using Bessel's correction for an unbiased estimate under common assumptions.
- Standard deviation is the square root of variance: σ = √σ². Variance is in squared units; SD is in the original measurement units.
- Computational population formula: σ² = [ΣX²/N] − μ². The equivalent sample computational formula must use the appropriate n−1 correction.
- **Related dispersion measures:** range (maximum−minimum), interquartile range (Q3−Q1), mean deviation, variance and SD. IQR is less affected by extreme scores.
- **Exam trap:** Do not square the sum of deviations. The deviations from the mean sum to zero; it is the *squared individual deviations* that are summed.

## Question 6

### 1. Question and options

```text
6. A researcher is interested in testing the effectiveness of Pre-post intervention on a single group.
   The statistical test that could be used is:
   (A) Correlation
   (B) Chi square
   (C) ANOVA
   (D) ‘t’ test
```

### 2. Answer

**D**

### 3. Explanation

For the same participants measured before and after an intervention, use a paired-samples t-test (two time points). For three or more repeated occasions, consider repeated-measures ANOVA. A one-group pre-post design has threats such as history and maturation.

### 4. Relevant concepts and exam connections

**Choosing a test for pre–post intervention data**
- If the **same participants** are measured before and after a two-time-point intervention, observations are dependent; use a **paired-samples (dependent) t-test** when difference scores are suitably approximately normal and the outcome is continuous.
- Independent-samples t-test compares two unrelated groups. One-way repeated-measures ANOVA is commonly used for three or more measurements/conditions on the same participants; independent-groups ANOVA compares separate groups.
- Non-parametric alternatives: Wilcoxon signed-rank for two related samples; Mann–Whitney U for two independent samples; Friedman test for three or more related conditions; Kruskal–Wallis for three or more independent groups.
- A paired t statistic is based on difference scores: **t = mean difference / (SD of differences ÷ √n)**; degrees of freedom = n−1.
- **Design issue:** A single-group pre–post design is vulnerable to history, maturation, testing and regression-to-the-mean threats. A control group strengthens causal inference.

## Question 7

### 1. Question and options

```text
7. Which of the following is correct?
   (A) Test-retest reliability is determined by assessing the degree of relationship between scores on
      one half of a test with scores on the other half of the test.

                                                             
                           

    (B) Alternate forms reliability is determined by assessing the degree of relationship between scores
        on two different equivalent tests.
    (C) Split-half reliability is determined by assessing the degree of relationship between scores on the
        same test, administered on two different occasions.
    (D) Internal reliability is the extent to which the effect of the dependent variable is the result of the
        independent variable and not some other aspect of the study.
```

### 2. Answer

**B**

### 3. Explanation

Alternate-forms reliability correlates scores from two equivalent test versions. Test-retest uses the same test on two occasions; split-half correlates halves of one administration; internal consistency assesses coherence among items (e.g., Cronbach's alpha).

### 4. Relevant concepts and exam connections

**Types of reliability**
- **Test–retest:** administer the same test twice to the same people; correlate scores to estimate temporal stability. A long interval can introduce real change; a short interval can cause practice effects.
- **Alternate/parallel forms:** correlate scores from two equivalent versions, useful where repeating identical items would cause memory/practice effects. Forms should be comparable in content and difficulty.
- **Split-half:** divide one test into two comparable halves and correlate them. The Spearman–Brown prophecy formula adjusts the half-test correlation to estimate full-test reliability: **r_full = 2r_half/(1+r_half)**.
- **Internal consistency:** degree to which items intended to measure the same construct cohere; common indices include Cronbach's alpha and KR-20 for dichotomous items. High alpha does not prove unidimensionality.
- **Inter-rater:** agreement among observers. Reliability is consistency, whereas **validity** concerns whether the test measures what it is intended to measure.

## Question 9

### 1. Question and options

```text
9. Utility of Semantic differential scale is not contingent upon:
    (A) Finding appropriate adjective pairs
    (B) Meaning of the words
    (C) Length of the items in the scale
    (D) Contextual reference of the items
```

### 2. Answer

**C**

### 3. Explanation

Semantic differential scales use bipolar adjective pairs (e.g., good–bad, strong–weak). The length of the item is not the defining dependency. Osgood's dimensions are evaluation, potency and activity.

### 4. Relevant concepts and exam connections

**Semantic Differential Scale (Osgood)**
- Developed by **Charles E. Osgood, George Suci and Percy Tannenbaum** to measure the connotative meaning or attitude associated with a concept. Respondents rate a concept between bipolar adjective anchors, e.g. good–bad, strong–weak, active–passive.
- The classic three evaluative dimensions are **Evaluation** (good–bad), **Potency** (strong–weak) and **Activity** (active–passive).
- The scale is often presented as a series of 5- or 7-point ratings. Selecting appropriate, meaningful bipolar adjective pairs and providing a clear target/context are important. Item wording length is not its defining utility condition.
- **Compare:** Likert scale uses agreement with statements and sums ratings; Thurstone uses equal-appearing intervals established by judges; Guttman is cumulative/hierarchical.
- **Exam clue:** Osgood → semantic differential; Likert → summated rating; Thurstone → equal-appearing interval; Guttman → cumulative scale.

## Question 10

### 1. Question and options

```text
10. Which structure of the Limbic system is mostly responsible for controlling the emotional behaviour
    of fear?
    (A) Amygdala
    (B) Hippocampus
    (C) Cingulate gyrus
    (D) Mammilary bodies
```

### 2. Answer

**A**

### 3. Explanation

The amygdala is central to threat detection and fear learning. Hippocampus supports episodic/contextual memory; cingulate contributes to emotion and control; mammillary bodies are part of memory circuitry.

### 4. Relevant concepts and exam connections

**Amygdala and the limbic system**
- The **amygdala** is a group of nuclei in the medial temporal lobe, important for detecting biologically significant stimuli, threat processing, fear conditioning, emotional learning and modulating emotional memories. Fear is not controlled by one structure alone; it involves distributed networks.
- **Hippocampus:** formation/consolidation of declarative and episodic memories; represents contextual information and helps distinguish where/when a threat occurred.
- **Cingulate gyrus/cortex:** emotion, motivation, attention, conflict monitoring and pain-related processing. **Mammillary bodies** (hypothalamic region) participate in memory circuits, including the Papez circuit.
- Other associated structures: hypothalamus (autonomic/endocrine responses), thalamus (sensory relay), septal area and parts of prefrontal cortex (regulation and appraisal).
- **Exam contrast:** amygdala—emotional salience/fear learning; hippocampus—episodic/contextual memory. The limbic system is a useful functional grouping, not a single isolated “emotion centre.”

## Question 11

### 1. Question and options

```text
11. REM sleep is characterized by one of the following conditions.
     (A) PGO waves
     (B) ULPA
     (C) Flip-flop
     (D) Histamine
```

### 2. Answer

**A**

### 3. Explanation

REM sleep is associated with ponto-geniculo-occipital (PGO) waves. REM also features rapid eye movements, vivid dreaming, cortical activation and skeletal muscle atonia. NREM includes N1, N2 (spindles/K-complexes) and N3 slow-wave sleep.

### 4. Relevant concepts and exam connections

**REM sleep and sleep physiology**
- REM means **rapid eye movement** sleep. It is associated with vivid dreaming, rapid eye movements, activated/desynchronised EEG, variable autonomic activity and marked skeletal-muscle atonia (apart from eye and respiratory muscles).
- **PGO waves** are phasic ponto-geniculo-occipital waves described in REM sleep, named for brainstem pons, lateral geniculate region and occipital cortex.
- Non-REM sleep is divided into N1, N2 and N3. N2 features sleep spindles and K-complexes; N3 is slow-wave/deep sleep with prominent delta activity.
- Sleep cycles recur roughly every 90 minutes, with REM periods generally becoming longer toward morning; exact cycle length varies.
- **Compare:** histamine is a wake-promoting neurotransmitter; flip-flop models describe switching between sleep- and wake-promoting systems. REM is sometimes called paradoxical sleep because brain activity is high while skeletal muscles are inhibited.

## Question 12

### 1. Question and options

```text
12. Class is to the ________ property of measurement as time is to the ______ property of measurement
     (A) Magnitude, Identity
     (B) Equal unit size, magnitude
     (C) Identity, absolute zero
     (D) Absolute zero, equal unit size
```

### 2. Answer

**C**

### 3. Explanation

Nominal scales provide identity; ordinal add rank; interval adds equal units; ratio adds an absolute zero. Class membership is nominal. Elapsed time is ratio, while clock time (e.g., 2 p.m.) has an arbitrary origin.

### 4. Relevant concepts and exam connections

**Four levels of measurement**
- **Nominal:** identity/category only; no ranking (e.g. blood group, class section).
- **Ordinal:** identity + order/rank, but gaps are not known equal (e.g. class rank, satisfaction categories).
- **Interval:** identity + order + equal units, but no true/absolute zero (e.g. Celsius temperature; zero does not mean no temperature).
- **Ratio:** all previous properties + an absolute zero, allowing meaningful ratios (e.g. elapsed time, weight, height, reaction time).
- Therefore class membership is nominal; elapsed time is ratio. Clock time is more complicated and should not be confused with elapsed duration.
- **Exam use:** nominal—mode/chi-square; ordinal—median/rank methods; interval/ratio—means, SD and many parametric procedures when assumptions are met. Measurement level guides, but does not mechanically dictate, statistical choice.

## Question 13

### 1. Question and options

```text
13. A genetic inability to metabolize amino acid phenylalanine leads to one of the following Conditions.
     (A) Down's syndrome
     (B) Autism
     (C) Cerebral Palsy
     (D) PKU
```

### 2. Answer

**D**

### 3. Explanation

Phenylketonuria (PKU) is an inherited metabolic disorder commonly caused by deficient phenylalanine hydroxylase, leading to phenylalanine accumulation. Newborn screening and early dietary management prevent serious neurodevelopmental effects.

### 4. Relevant concepts and exam connections

**Phenylketonuria (PKU)**
- PKU is an inherited metabolic disorder, usually autosomal recessive, most often caused by deficiency of **phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH)**. The enzyme normally converts phenylalanine to tyrosine; deficiency leads to excessive phenylalanine and risk of neurotoxicity if untreated.
- Newborn screening allows early detection. Treatment generally involves a lifelong controlled low-phenylalanine diet, specialised medical foods and monitoring; management is specialist-led.
- Untreated PKU can cause intellectual disability, developmental delay, seizures and behavioural/neurological difficulties. Early treatment greatly reduces these risks.
- **Differentiate:** Down syndrome is usually trisomy 21 (chromosomal); cerebral palsy is a group of movement/posture disorders due to early brain disturbance; autism spectrum disorder is a neurodevelopmental condition with social-communication differences and restricted/repetitive patterns.
- **Exam clue:** PKU—amino-acid metabolism/phenylalanine; Down—chromosome 21; Turner—45,X; Klinefelter—47,XXY; Fragile X—FMR1 expansion.

## Question 14

### 1. Question and options

```text
14. A person who had experienced an intensive negative emotion fails to recollect the emotional event.
    This is known as:
     (A) Forgetting due to interference
     (B) Retrieval failure
     (C) Motivated Forgetting
     (D) Forgetting due to decay
```

### 2. Answer

**C**

### 3. Explanation

Motivated forgetting refers broadly to memory avoidance or inaccessibility associated with psychological motives. Distinguish interference (other learning disrupts recall), decay (weakening over time) and retrieval failure (stored information cannot currently be accessed).

### 4. Relevant concepts and exam connections

**Motivated forgetting**
- Motivated forgetting refers to failure to recall information in connection with psychological motives, such as avoiding distressing thoughts or memories. In psychodynamic language, **repression** is an unconscious exclusion of unacceptable material; **suppression** is a conscious attempt to put something out of mind.
- It should not be assumed that every inaccessible traumatic memory has been repressed. Memory is reconstructive and can be influenced by attention, encoding, retrieval cues, suggestion and ordinary forgetting.
- **Interference:** proactive interference—old learning disrupts new learning; retroactive interference—new learning disrupts recall of old learning. **Decay** refers to weakening over time, especially when memory traces are not used (theories differ). **Retrieval failure** occurs when stored information cannot be accessed without suitable cues.
- **Exam distinction:** motivated forgetting is motive-related; interference is competition between memories; retrieval failure is cue/access difficulty; encoding failure means information was not adequately stored in the first place.

## Question 15

### 1. Question and options

```text
15. The tendency of a consumer to decide on buying a product that was repeatedly appearing on T.V.
    screen is attributed to
     (A) Effectiveness of the product
     (B) Price of the product
     (C) Subliminal perception
     (D) Utility of the product
```

### 2. Answer

**No valid option; the supplied key selects C**

### 3. Explanation

The answer key selects subliminal perception, but repeated television exposure is more conventionally explained by the mere-exposure effect: familiarity from repetition can increase liking. Subliminal perception means processing below conscious awareness; repetition need not be subliminal.

### 4. Relevant concepts and exam connections

**Repeated exposure, subliminal perception and consumer behaviour**
- **Mere-exposure effect (Robert Zajonc):** repeated exposure to a stimulus can increase liking or familiarity, even without detailed conscious evaluation. This is the conventional explanation for a product becoming familiar after repeated advertisements.
- **Subliminal perception** refers to processing of stimuli presented below the threshold of conscious awareness. It is not the same as an advertisement simply being repeated or noticed consciously.
- Consumer decisions can also be influenced by brand familiarity, classical conditioning, social proof, source credibility, affect, price, perceived quality and availability heuristics.
- **Classical conditioning in advertising:** a brand (initially neutral stimulus) is paired with positive music/images, potentially acquiring positive affective associations. **Operant conditioning:** rewards/loyalty points can reinforce purchasing.
- **Source-key caution:** the supplied key marks subliminal perception, but the wording “repeatedly appearing” more directly supports mere exposure. Learn both terms and do not treat the key's label as a precise definition.

## Question 16

### 1. Question and options

```text
16. Brain lesions of sub cortical regions produced by passing electrical current through an insulated
    electrode is called:
     (A) Excitotoxic lesions
     (B) Radio frequency lesions
     (C) Sham lesions
     (D) Excitatory lesions
```

### 2. Answer

**B**

### 3. Explanation

Radio-frequency lesions use current through an insulated electrode to generate local heat and damage targeted brain tissue. Stereotaxic coordinates guide placement. Excitotoxic lesions use neurotoxins; sham lesions mimic surgery without the active lesion.

### 4. Relevant concepts and exam connections

**Radio-frequency brain lesions and stereotaxic methods**
- A radio-frequency (RF) lesion is produced by passing current through an electrode, generating heat that damages a targeted brain region. The electrode is insulated along its shaft so the lesion is concentrated near its exposed tip.
- **Stereotaxic apparatus** holds the animal's head in a fixed position. A stereotaxic atlas provides coordinates to target a brain structure reproducibly.
- **Excitotoxic lesion:** a neurotoxin (e.g. ibotenic acid or kainic acid) damages cell bodies near the injection site while often sparing fibres of passage. RF lesions can damage cell bodies and fibres passing through the area.
- **Sham lesion/surgery:** control procedure mimics surgery without producing the intended brain damage, helping control for effects of anaesthesia and handling.
- **Research ethics:** animal studies require justification, minimisation of suffering, appropriate oversight and refinement/reduction/replacement principles.

## Question 17

### 1. Question and options

```text
17. A person dressed in a gorilla suit walked unnoticed through a group of students playing basket ball.
    How can this phenomenon be described?
     (A) Change of attention
     (B) Attention loss
     (C) Inattention blindness
     (D) Attention lapse
```

### 2. Answer

**C**

### 3. Explanation

Inattentional blindness occurs when an unexpected stimulus is not noticed because attention is occupied by another task. It differs from change blindness, where a change between scenes is missed.

### 4. Relevant concepts and exam connections

**Inattentional blindness**
- Inattentional blindness is failure to notice a visible but unexpected stimulus because attention is engaged elsewhere. The classic **Invisible Gorilla** demonstration is associated with Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris (1999): observers counting passes often miss a person in a gorilla suit.
- **Change blindness:** failure to detect a change in a scene, often across a brief interruption or visual disruption. **Attentional blink:** reduced ability to report a second target when it appears shortly after the first in a rapid stream.
- **Selective attention** prioritises some inputs while filtering others; **divided attention** attempts to handle multiple tasks; **sustained attention/vigilance** maintains focus over time.
- These findings show that visual presence does not guarantee conscious awareness. Expectations, task demands, salience and attentional load affect detection.
- **Exam trap:** gorilla-not-seen = inattentional blindness; unnoticed scene alteration = change blindness.

## Question 18

### 1. Question and options

```text
18. The light sensation from the left and right visual field travels to the visual cortex through:
     (A) Lateral geniculate nucleus
     (B) Superior colliculus
     (C) Optic radiations
     (D) Pulvinar nucleus
```

### 2. Answer

**Exclude from single-answer scoring; the supplied key accepts A and C**

### 3. Explanation

Visual information travels from retina through optic nerve/chiasm/tract to the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN), then via optic radiations to primary visual cortex. The key lists both LGN and optic radiations. Nasal retinal fibres cross at the optic chiasm.

### 4. Relevant concepts and exam connections

**Visual pathway from retina to visual cortex**
- Main route: photoreceptors → bipolar cells → retinal ganglion cells → optic nerve → optic chiasm → optic tract → **lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) of thalamus** → optic radiations → primary visual cortex (V1, occipital lobe).
- At the optic chiasm, fibres from the nasal half of each retina cross; temporal retinal fibres remain on the same side. Consequently, each cerebral hemisphere processes the **contralateral visual field**.
- The **superior colliculus** contributes to orienting eye/head movements and visual attention; the pulvinar participates in attentional and visual processing. These are not the principal relay route to V1.
- **Two broad cortical streams:** ventral “what” pathway toward temporal cortex supports object/form recognition; dorsal “where/how” pathway toward parietal cortex supports spatial processing and visually guided action.
- The paper's key accepts both LGN and optic radiations (A and C), which are successive parts of the pathway.

## Question 19

### 1. Question and options

```text
19. Step by step interaction of operating a mechanical gadget is an example of :
     (A) Trouble shooting
     (B) Algorithm
     (C) Heuristic approach
     (D) Means-end analysis
```

### 2. Answer

**B**

### 3. Explanation

An algorithm is a systematic sequence of steps for solving a problem. Heuristics are shortcuts that are faster but can produce errors. Means–ends analysis reduces the gap between current and goal states through subgoals.

### 4. Relevant concepts and exam connections

**Problem-solving strategies**
- **Algorithm:** a definite, systematic sequence of steps that guarantees a solution when correctly applied to a well-defined problem, though it may be slow. A recipe or troubleshooting checklist can be algorithmic.

- **Means–ends analysis:** compare current state with goal state, identify differences, and select subgoals/operators to reduce the gap.
- **Trial and error:** attempt possible solutions until one works. **Insight:** sudden restructuring/understanding of a problem. **Analogy:** transfer a solution from a similar previous problem.

### 4. Relevant concepts and exam connections

**Moderation vs mediation**
- A **moderator** changes the strength or direction of the relationship between a predictor and an outcome. Here gender is a moderator if the social-support/well-being association differs across gender groups. Statistically this is tested with an interaction term (social support × gender).

### 4. Relevant concepts and exam connections

**p-values and non-significant findings**
- If a result is not statistically significant at α = .05, then p > .05. Failure to support a hypothesis does not by itself prove the result was non-significant.
- A p-value is the probability, assuming the null hypothesis and model assumptions, of obtaining data at least as extreme as those observed. It is **not** the probability that the null hypothesis is true, nor the probability the result occurred “by chance.”

```text
100. Which type of statistical analysis was used?
      (A) Simple linear regression
      (B) Biserial correlation
      (C) Multiple regression
      (D) Log linear correlation
```

### 2. Answer

**C**

### 3. Explanation

Multiple regression predicts a continuous criterion (well-being) from two or more predictors (social support and gender, potentially their interaction). A moderation test includes an interaction term; for categorical gender, dummy coding is commonly used.

### 4. Relevant concepts and exam connections

**Multiple regression and moderation analysis**
- **Multiple linear regression** predicts a continuous outcome from two or more predictors. General form: **Y = b₀ + b₁X₁ + b₂X₂ + … + ε**. Here well-being is continuous; social support and gender can be predictors.
- To test moderation, include an interaction term: **Y = b₀ + b₁X + b₂M + b₃(X×M) + ε**. A significant b₃ suggests the association between X and Y differs by moderator M, subject to assumptions and interpretation.
- **Simple linear regression** has one predictor; **multiple regression** has two or more. **Biserial/point-biserial correlation** are correlation coefficients, not general multivariable prediction models. **Log-linear analysis** is generally used for categorical count data/contingency tables.
- Check linearity, independent errors, homoscedasticity, multicollinearity, residual distribution for inference and appropriate coding of categorical predictors.
- **Exam clue:** continuous outcome + multiple predictors = multiple regression; interaction term = moderation test.
